What is financial literacy?

Financial literacy is the knowledge and confidence to make sound decisions about earning, spending, saving, borrowing and investing money.

It is not about being wealthy or knowing every product. It is about understanding enough to read a contract, compare options, weigh risk and plan ahead, so that money works for your goals rather than against them.

Financial literacy sits alongside economic literacy. Economic literacy explains the wider forces, such as inflation, interest rates and the choices governments make. Together they let people make informed decisions in their own lives and take part in public debate.

44Personal financeBudgetingPlan spending and saving with real numbers.+
Learning goal

Understand budgeting, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

A budget sets out expected income and spending over a period. Its purpose is to help you decide how to use limited money, not to shame you for spending. A workable budget distinguishes fixed essentials, variable essentials, discretionary purchases, debt commitments and saving goals.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Begin with after-tax income, track real spending for a few weeks, and assign amounts to categories. Include irregular bills as monthly provisions. A 50/30/20 split is one possible starting guide, not a standard everyone can afford to meet.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Budgeting is a repeated decision, not a one-time spreadsheet. Compare planned and actual results monthly, identify why a category exceeded its limit, and change the plan if prices or priorities shift. Build a small cash buffer where possible.

WORKED EXAMPLE

If monthly take-home pay is $2,000, essentials cost $1,100, fixed debt payments $150 and saving $250, the remaining $500 can cover flexible spending. Spending $600 in that final category would create a $100 shortfall unless another allocation changes.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A zero-dollar balance at the end of the month is not automatically a sign of good budgeting if it leaves no room for unexpected expenses.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which is an example of planning for an irregular expense?

  1. Ignoring annual insurance until it arrives
  2. Saving one twelfth of the annual insurance each month
  3. Treating the insurance bill as discretionary
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Saving one twelfth of the annual insurance each month. Converting a yearly obligation to monthly savings reduces the risk of a sudden cash shortfall.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why budgeting with after-tax rather than pre-tax income is more accurate.

See a model response

Income tax and other deductions reduce the cash actually received. Using gross pay overstates the money available for bills, saving and spending, making the budget less reliable.

08Personal financeBuy now, pay laterShort-term instalment credit with its own risks.+
Learning goal

Understand buy now, pay later, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Buy now, pay later (BNPL) lets you buy an item immediately and pay in instalments. The arrangement can feel smaller than paying the full price upfront, but it still creates a repayment obligation. Some products charge late fees or other charges.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Put every scheduled instalment into your budget, not just the payment due today. If several purchases overlap, their combined repayments can become hard to manage. Compare BNPL with waiting and saving, lay-by or a debit-card purchase.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Australian rules and provider obligations have changed, so terms and consumer protections should be checked with ASIC Moneysmart and the provider. The sensible question is not only “Can I make the first payment?” but “Can I make every payment on time?”

WORKED EXAMPLE

A $240 purchase split into four equal payments requires $60 each time. If you make three similar purchases at once, the combined instalment per round becomes $180, before any fees or other bills.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Interest free” is not the same as “cost free” or “risk free”; missed payments can have consequences.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which is the safest first check before using BNPL?

  1. Whether every scheduled instalment fits your budget
  2. Whether approval is instant
  3. Whether the first instalment is small
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Whether every scheduled instalment fits your budget. Affordability depends on the total repayment schedule, not the convenience of the initial payment.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain how BNPL can lead to overspending even if no interest is charged.

See a model response

Splitting a price into smaller amounts can make each purchase seem cheap. If multiple instalments overlap, the combined future obligation reduces cash available for essentials and can trigger late charges or missed payments.

02Personal financeCash flowThe money moving in and out of your accounts each month.+
Learning goal

Understand cash flow, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Cash flow is money entering and leaving an account over a period. Inflows include wages, transfers and interest; outflows include rent, food, transport and loan repayments. Positive cash flow means more cash enters than leaves in that period. It does not necessarily mean someone is wealthy.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Map a typical month using actual bank transactions. Separate regular bills from irregular costs such as annual insurance and school expenses. Convert irregular costs into a monthly amount, then set that money aside. An annual $600 bill is effectively $50 per month even though it is paid once.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Cash flow is especially important when income fluctuates. Someone may earn enough across a year but struggle in a quiet month if they have not built a buffer. Forecasting expected cash balances reveals problems before a payment is due.

WORKED EXAMPLE

In one month Maya earns $1,900 from casual shifts and spends $950 on essentials, $400 on discretionary purchases and $250 on transport. Net cash flow = $1,900 − $1,600 = +$300. If a $480 annual bill falls due, that month becomes −$180 unless she planned for it.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Do not confuse income with cash flow: gross wages before tax are not the money actually available to spend.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

A person has $2,400 in monthly cash inflows and $2,650 in outflows. Their cash flow is…

  1. Positive $250
  2. Negative $250
  3. Zero
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Negative $250. Outflows exceed inflows by $250, so cash is being drawn down or borrowed.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why budgeting for annual expenses improves cash-flow management.

See a model response

Annual expenses create large outflows in one month. Saving a smaller amount each month smooths the impact and makes the cash balance more predictable, reducing the chance that the person must borrow or miss a payment.

47Personal financeCredit and debtUnderstand borrowing costs and repayment decisions.+
Learning goal

Understand credit and debt, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Credit allows someone to receive money or goods now and pay later. Debt creates a future repayment obligation, often including interest and fees. Credit can fund useful long-lived assets, but repayment pressure can damage cash flow when borrowing exceeds capacity.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Before borrowing, compare the annual percentage rate, fees, repayment frequency, term and total cost. Minimum credit card payments may leave a balance outstanding for a long time. Paying debts with high interest rates first often reduces the total interest paid, but account for penalties and urgent obligations.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Debt is not inherently good or bad: its effects depend on purpose, price and affordability. Borrowing for an asset that produces income is different from borrowing for a quickly consumed item, though neither guarantees a favourable outcome.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A $1,000 credit card balance with a hypothetical 20% annual interest rate accrues roughly $16.67 interest in a 30-day-equivalent month using a simple monthly estimate of 20% ÷ 12, before payments and fees. The exact charge depends on the card’s daily-interest rules.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

The minimum repayment required by a lender is not a guarantee that the debt is affordable or will be repaid quickly.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which piece of information most helps compare two loans?

  1. Total interest and fees over the loan term
  2. The colour of the lender’s app
  3. Only the first repayment
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Total interest and fees over the loan term. Total borrowing cost captures interest, fees and time, not just the initial instalment.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why paying only the minimum on a high-interest credit card can be expensive.

See a model response

Because much of each repayment can go toward interest, principal falls slowly. Interest then continues to accrue on the remaining balance, increasing the time and total cost needed to clear the debt.

07Personal financeCredit scoresA number lenders use to judge how reliably you repay.+
Learning goal

Understand credit scores, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

In Australia, credit reporting bodies compile credit reports, while different providers may calculate different credit scores. Lenders may consider this information alongside income, expenses and existing debt when assessing applications. A score alone does not guarantee loan approval.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Reports can include credit enquiries, account details, repayment history and certain defaults. Paying obligations on time and checking reports for errors can help protect your credit record. You can request a free report from Australian credit reporting bodies under applicable rules.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

A credit application may be recorded even if you are rejected. Different lenders apply different policies, so two people with the same score may receive different outcomes. Use official Australian guidance rather than assuming American credit-score advice applies here.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A lender assesses a car-loan application. The applicant has paid previous instalments on time but also has a large existing loan. Repayment history may be favourable, yet affordability can still be a concern after accounting for income and expenses.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Checking your own credit report is different from repeatedly applying for new credit. Avoid unnecessary applications just to see what you might be approved for.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

What is the most accurate description of a credit score?

  1. A guarantee of loan approval
  2. One indicator lenders may use alongside affordability information
  3. A measure of your yearly salary
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: One indicator lenders may use alongside affordability information. Lenders assess multiple factors, including the ability to make repayments, not merely a score.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why someone with a good credit score might still be refused a mortgage.

See a model response

A strong credit score can indicate a favourable credit record, but the lender also considers serviceability, income, debts, expenses and its own lending standards. If proposed repayments are unaffordable, the application may be refused.

56Personal financeHECS-HELP and student debtHow Australian study loans and indexation differ from bank loans.+
Learning goal

Explain hecs-help and student debt, apply it to a practical decision and identify a trade-off.

01 / LEARN

The concept

HECS-HELP is an Australian Government loan that can cover eligible student contribution amounts at university. It is not a conventional bank loan with a standard commercial interest rate. Eligible debts may be indexed over time, and compulsory repayments generally depend on repayment income under the rules for the relevant year.

02 / LEARN

How it works

A loan balance can change when further study fees are added, indexation is applied or repayments are credited. Indexation adjusts the balance under legislated arrangements; it should not be confused with a credit-card interest charge. Repayment rules and thresholds change, so consult StudyAssist and the ATO for the year you are planning for.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Compare the cost of a degree with expected study choices and future earning capacity, rather than assuming all debts work the same way. When budgeting after graduation, keep HELP repayment obligations distinct from ordinary living costs and check how payroll withholding may affect take-home pay.

WORKED EXAMPLE

An illustrative $30,000 HELP balance exposed to a 2% annual indexation adjustment would increase by $600 to $30,600 before any eligible repayments or new borrowing. This 2% is a teaching assumption, not a forecast or current official indexation rate.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

HELP rules, rates and thresholds are policy-dependent. Never rely on last year’s threshold to estimate a future compulsory repayment.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

What is a key difference between HELP and an ordinary fixed-payment personal loan?

  1. HELP always has zero balance growth
  2. HELP compulsory repayments are linked to income rules
  3. HELP can only be repaid in weekly instalments
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: HELP compulsory repayments are linked to income rules. Compulsory HELP repayments generally depend on repayment income once the relevant annual rules apply.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Think it through

Explain why choosing a university course based only on the headline HELP balance could be misleading.

See a model response

The balance is only one dimension. A student should also consider course outcomes, non-tuition living costs, their circumstances, future income uncertainty and the rules governing indexation and repayment.

61Personal financeIdentity theft and account securityProtect passwords, personal information and access to your money.+
Learning goal

Explain identity theft and account security, apply it to a practical decision and identify a trade-off.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Financial identity theft happens when someone misuses personal information to access accounts, impersonate you or attempt transactions. Risks range from stolen passwords and fraudulent links to convincing calls that request one-time verification codes. Good security is an ongoing routine, not a single software purchase.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Use distinct long passwords or a password manager, enable multifactor authentication, keep devices updated and avoid sharing one-time codes. Independently open the official banking app rather than follow an unexpected link. Monitor account notifications and act quickly if you notice a suspicious payment or change to account details.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

If you receive an unexpected call claiming to be from your bank, end it and contact the bank through a known official number or the app. If a code or password has been disclosed, secure the account immediately and tell the institution; keep records of the incident.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A text says your account is frozen and asks for a six-digit code. You have not requested a login. Do not send the code. Open your bank’s app independently, check for official notices and report the message through the bank’s published process.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A familiar logo, caller ID or message thread is not proof that a request is genuine. Scammers can imitate these signals.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

What is the safest response to an unexpected request for a bank login code?

  1. Share it because the caller knows your name
  2. Verify through your bank’s official app or published contact details
  3. Forward the code to a friend for checking
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Verify through your bank’s official app or published contact details. Independent verification avoids trusting details supplied by a potentially fraudulent contact.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Think it through

Explain why multifactor authentication helps but does not eliminate financial fraud.

See a model response

Multifactor authentication adds another barrier to unauthorised account access. However, a scammer may still persuade someone to approve a login or transfer, so independent verification and careful transaction checks remain necessary.

52Personal financeInsuranceProtect against large, uncertain financial losses.+
Learning goal

Understand insurance, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Insurance is a contract that transfers specified financial risks to an insurer in exchange for premiums. It helps protect against large, uncertain losses such as major property damage or certain medical expenses, depending on the product and its terms.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Compare the premium, excess, coverage limit, waiting periods and exclusions. A higher excess often reduces the premium but means the policyholder pays more when making a claim. The right coverage depends on what losses would be difficult to absorb personally.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Insurance is about managing severe risks, not making money. Read the product disclosure statement and confirm that coverage matches the actual situation. Policies can change, so review them when assets, work or living arrangements change.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Policy A costs $900 per year with a $500 excess. Policy B costs $650 with a $1,000 excess. If a covered claim occurs, B costs $250 less in annual premium but requires $500 more excess. Whether it is preferable depends on the risk and your ability to pay the excess.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

The cheapest premium is not necessarily best: inadequate coverage can leave a large loss uninsured.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

What is an insurance excess?

  1. An amount the policyholder pays toward a claim
  2. The yearly return on the policy
  3. A guaranteed refund of premiums
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: An amount the policyholder pays toward a claim. The excess is the policyholder’s contribution to a covered claim under the policy.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why a person might willingly pay a higher insurance premium for a lower excess.

See a model response

A lower excess reduces the amount paid out of pocket when making a covered claim. Someone with limited emergency savings may prefer that certainty even if the annual premium is higher.

58Personal financeLoan comparison rates and total costWhy the lowest interest rate may not mean the cheapest loan.+
Learning goal

Explain loan comparison rates and total cost, apply it to a practical decision and identify a trade-off.

01 / LEARN

The concept

The interest rate is the charge for borrowing a principal balance. Fees, loan term and repayment structure also affect the total cost. An advertised rate can therefore look attractive while the complete loan is more expensive than an alternative. A comparison rate can help show certain interest-and-fee costs on a standardised basis for eligible consumer credit products.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Check the advertised interest rate, comparison rate where supplied, application and ongoing fees, loan term, repayment frequency and any early-repayment conditions. Compare like with like: a longer loan often reduces the scheduled monthly payment but increases the total interest paid when other variables stay constant.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

A useful comparison looks at total repayments and whether the debt leaves sufficient room for essentials and unexpected expenses. The comparison rate is only one measure: it cannot capture every product feature or every possible fee.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Borrowing $10,000 over five years at 8% per year with monthly repayments of roughly $203 costs about $2,166 in interest if the rate stays constant, before fees. An added $10 monthly fee would contribute another $600 over the 60 months. Both figures matter when comparing offers.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Do not compare loans using monthly repayment alone. Extending the term can make the payment look affordable while increasing total borrowing costs.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which comparison is most useful when choosing between two loans?

  1. Only the advertised monthly repayment
  2. Total repayments, fees, interest and key conditions
  3. The amount shown in the advertisement headline
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Total repayments, fees, interest and key conditions. A full comparison considers the amount repaid over time as well as important loan terms and fees.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Think it through

Explain why a loan with a lower interest rate could still cost more than a competing loan.

See a model response

The lower-rate loan could charge higher application or ongoing fees or have a longer repayment term. Its total repayments may therefore exceed the alternative even if the quoted rate is lower.

09Personal financeMortgages explainedA loan to buy property, secured against it.+
Learning goal

Understand mortgages explained, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

A mortgage is a property loan secured against the property being purchased. The borrower owes the principal and usually pays interest on the outstanding balance. If repayments cannot be met, the lender may ultimately enforce the security under applicable law.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Principal-and-interest repayments gradually reduce debt. Interest-only arrangements may lower payments initially while leaving principal unchanged. Fixed rates lock in an agreed rate for a period; variable rates can change. Loan term, deposit size, fees and comparison rates all affect total cost.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Test whether the loan remains manageable if rates or expenses rise. A larger deposit can reduce borrowing and sometimes avoid lenders mortgage insurance, but buying also brings stamp duty, maintenance, insurance and other costs.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Borrow $400,000 at a hypothetical 6% per year. The first month’s interest is roughly $400,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = $2,000. A $2,400 principal-and-interest payment would reduce the principal by about $400 in that first month, ignoring fees and exact daily-interest calculations.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A mortgage payment is not all interest, and a lower monthly payment can mean a much higher lifetime interest cost if the loan runs longer.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which loan feature usually lowers the initial monthly repayment but slows principal reduction?

  1. Principal-and-interest repayments
  2. Interest-only repayments
  3. A larger deposit with the same rate
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Interest-only repayments. Interest-only payments do not pay down principal during the interest-only period.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why comparing home loans using only the advertised interest rate can be misleading.

See a model response

Fees, rate conditions, loan term and features alter the total cost. Two loans with similar rates can produce different repayments and lifetime interest, so borrowers should compare total repayments and relevant comparison rates.

01Personal financeNet worthWhat you own minus what you owe.+
Learning goal

Understand net worth, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Net worth is a snapshot of financial position: the value of assets minus outstanding liabilities. Assets might include bank balances, shares, superannuation and a home; liabilities include home loans, credit card debt and other loans. It is not the same as income: a high earner can still have negative net worth.

02 / LEARN

How it works

To calculate it, choose one date, estimate the current value of each asset and subtract the amount owed on each debt. Use conservative estimates for items that are difficult to sell, and do not count future wages as an asset. If a home is included at its market value, also include the mortgage still owing.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Tracking net worth over time can reveal whether saving, investing and debt repayment are building financial resilience. A negative balance is not automatically a failure: someone early in their working life may have education debt and few assets. Cash available for bills still matters, even when net worth is high.

A subsidy may improve environmental sustainability by reducing local emissions and can improve equity if it expands affordable public transport. However, the cost to taxpayers and opportunity cost of other spending reduce its efficiency unless benefits exceed costs. I would support a targeted subsidy where expected public benefits are demonstrably large.
