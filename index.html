What is financial literacy?

Financial literacy is the knowledge and confidence to make sound decisions about earning, spending, saving, borrowing and investing money.

It is not about being wealthy or knowing every product. It is about understanding enough to read a contract, compare options, weigh risk and plan ahead, so that money works for your goals rather than against them.

Financial literacy sits alongside economic literacy. Economic literacy explains the wider forces, such as inflation, interest rates and the choices governments make. Together they let people make informed decisions in their own lives and take part in public debate.

44Personal financeBudgetingPlan spending and saving with real numbers.+
Learning goal

Understand budgeting, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

A budget sets out expected income and spending over a period. Its purpose is to help you decide how to use limited money, not to shame you for spending. A workable budget distinguishes fixed essentials, variable essentials, discretionary purchases, debt commitments and saving goals.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Begin with after-tax income, track real spending for a few weeks, and assign amounts to categories. Include irregular bills as monthly provisions. A 50/30/20 split is one possible starting guide, not a standard everyone can afford to meet.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Budgeting is a repeated decision, not a one-time spreadsheet. Compare planned and actual results monthly, identify why a category exceeded its limit, and change the plan if prices or priorities shift. Build a small cash buffer where possible.

WORKED EXAMPLE

If monthly take-home pay is $2,000, essentials cost $1,100, fixed debt payments $150 and saving $250, the remaining $500 can cover flexible spending. Spending $600 in that final category would create a $100 shortfall unless another allocation changes.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A zero-dollar balance at the end of the month is not automatically a sign of good budgeting if it leaves no room for unexpected expenses.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which is an example of planning for an irregular expense?

  1. Ignoring annual insurance until it arrives
  2. Saving one twelfth of the annual insurance each month
  3. Treating the insurance bill as discretionary
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Saving one twelfth of the annual insurance each month. Converting a yearly obligation to monthly savings reduces the risk of a sudden cash shortfall.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why budgeting with after-tax rather than pre-tax income is more accurate.

See a model response

Income tax and other deductions reduce the cash actually received. Using gross pay overstates the money available for bills, saving and spending, making the budget less reliable.

08Personal financeBuy now, pay laterShort-term instalment credit with its own risks.+
Learning goal

Understand buy now, pay later, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Buy now, pay later (BNPL) lets you buy an item immediately and pay in instalments. The arrangement can feel smaller than paying the full price upfront, but it still creates a repayment obligation. Some products charge late fees or other charges.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Put every scheduled instalment into your budget, not just the payment due today. If several purchases overlap, their combined repayments can become hard to manage. Compare BNPL with waiting and saving, lay-by or a debit-card purchase.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Australian rules and provider obligations have changed, so terms and consumer protections should be checked with ASIC Moneysmart and the provider. The sensible question is not only “Can I make the first payment?” but “Can I make every payment on time?”

WORKED EXAMPLE

A $240 purchase split into four equal payments requires $60 each time. If you make three similar purchases at once, the combined instalment per round becomes $180, before any fees or other bills.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

“Interest free” is not the same as “cost free” or “risk free”; missed payments can have consequences.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which is the safest first check before using BNPL?

  1. Whether every scheduled instalment fits your budget
  2. Whether approval is instant
  3. Whether the first instalment is small
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Whether every scheduled instalment fits your budget. Affordability depends on the total repayment schedule, not the convenience of the initial payment.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain how BNPL can lead to overspending even if no interest is charged.

See a model response

Splitting a price into smaller amounts can make each purchase seem cheap. If multiple instalments overlap, the combined future obligation reduces cash available for essentials and can trigger late charges or missed payments.

02Personal financeCash flowThe money moving in and out of your accounts each month.+
Learning goal

Understand cash flow, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Cash flow is money entering and leaving an account over a period. Inflows include wages, transfers and interest; outflows include rent, food, transport and loan repayments. Positive cash flow means more cash enters than leaves in that period. It does not necessarily mean someone is wealthy.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Map a typical month using actual bank transactions. Separate regular bills from irregular costs such as annual insurance and school expenses. Convert irregular costs into a monthly amount, then set that money aside. An annual $600 bill is effectively $50 per month even though it is paid once.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Cash flow is especially important when income fluctuates. Someone may earn enough across a year but struggle in a quiet month if they have not built a buffer. Forecasting expected cash balances reveals problems before a payment is due.

WORKED EXAMPLE

In one month Maya earns $1,900 from casual shifts and spends $950 on essentials, $400 on discretionary purchases and $250 on transport. Net cash flow = $1,900 − $1,600 = +$300. If a $480 annual bill falls due, that month becomes −$180 unless she planned for it.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Do not confuse income with cash flow: gross wages before tax are not the money actually available to spend.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

A person has $2,400 in monthly cash inflows and $2,650 in outflows. Their cash flow is…

  1. Positive $250
  2. Negative $250
  3. Zero
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Negative $250. Outflows exceed inflows by $250, so cash is being drawn down or borrowed.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why budgeting for annual expenses improves cash-flow management.

See a model response

Annual expenses create large outflows in one month. Saving a smaller amount each month smooths the impact and makes the cash balance more predictable, reducing the chance that the person must borrow or miss a payment.

47Personal financeCredit and debtUnderstand borrowing costs and repayment decisions.+
Learning goal

Understand credit and debt, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Credit allows someone to receive money or goods now and pay later. Debt creates a future repayment obligation, often including interest and fees. Credit can fund useful long-lived assets, but repayment pressure can damage cash flow when borrowing exceeds capacity.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Before borrowing, compare the annual percentage rate, fees, repayment frequency, term and total cost. Minimum credit card payments may leave a balance outstanding for a long time. Paying debts with high interest rates first often reduces the total interest paid, but account for penalties and urgent obligations.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Debt is not inherently good or bad: its effects depend on purpose, price and affordability. Borrowing for an asset that produces income is different from borrowing for a quickly consumed item, though neither guarantees a favourable outcome.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A $1,000 credit card balance with a hypothetical 20% annual interest rate accrues roughly $16.67 interest in a 30-day-equivalent month using a simple monthly estimate of 20% ÷ 12, before payments and fees. The exact charge depends on the card’s daily-interest rules.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

The minimum repayment required by a lender is not a guarantee that the debt is affordable or will be repaid quickly.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which piece of information most helps compare two loans?

  1. Total interest and fees over the loan term
  2. The colour of the lender’s app
  3. Only the first repayment
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Total interest and fees over the loan term. Total borrowing cost captures interest, fees and time, not just the initial instalment.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why paying only the minimum on a high-interest credit card can be expensive.

See a model response

Because much of each repayment can go toward interest, principal falls slowly. Interest then continues to accrue on the remaining balance, increasing the time and total cost needed to clear the debt.

07Personal financeCredit scoresA number lenders use to judge how reliably you repay.+
Learning goal

Understand credit scores, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

In Australia, credit reporting bodies compile credit reports, while different providers may calculate different credit scores. Lenders may consider this information alongside income, expenses and existing debt when assessing applications. A score alone does not guarantee loan approval.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Reports can include credit enquiries, account details, repayment history and certain defaults. Paying obligations on time and checking reports for errors can help protect your credit record. You can request a free report from Australian credit reporting bodies under applicable rules.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

A credit application may be recorded even if you are rejected. Different lenders apply different policies, so two people with the same score may receive different outcomes. Use official Australian guidance rather than assuming American credit-score advice applies here.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A lender assesses a car-loan application. The applicant has paid previous instalments on time but also has a large existing loan. Repayment history may be favourable, yet affordability can still be a concern after accounting for income and expenses.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Checking your own credit report is different from repeatedly applying for new credit. Avoid unnecessary applications just to see what you might be approved for.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

What is the most accurate description of a credit score?

  1. A guarantee of loan approval
  2. One indicator lenders may use alongside affordability information
  3. A measure of your yearly salary
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: One indicator lenders may use alongside affordability information. Lenders assess multiple factors, including the ability to make repayments, not merely a score.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why someone with a good credit score might still be refused a mortgage.

See a model response

A strong credit score can indicate a favourable credit record, but the lender also considers serviceability, income, debts, expenses and its own lending standards. If proposed repayments are unaffordable, the application may be refused.

52Personal financeInsuranceProtect against large, uncertain financial losses.+
Learning goal

Understand insurance, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Insurance is a contract that transfers specified financial risks to an insurer in exchange for premiums. It helps protect against large, uncertain losses such as major property damage or certain medical expenses, depending on the product and its terms.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Compare the premium, excess, coverage limit, waiting periods and exclusions. A higher excess often reduces the premium but means the policyholder pays more when making a claim. The right coverage depends on what losses would be difficult to absorb personally.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Insurance is about managing severe risks, not making money. Read the product disclosure statement and confirm that coverage matches the actual situation. Policies can change, so review them when assets, work or living arrangements change.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Policy A costs $900 per year with a $500 excess. Policy B costs $650 with a $1,000 excess. If a covered claim occurs, B costs $250 less in annual premium but requires $500 more excess. Whether it is preferable depends on the risk and your ability to pay the excess.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

The cheapest premium is not necessarily best: inadequate coverage can leave a large loss uninsured.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

What is an insurance excess?

  1. An amount the policyholder pays toward a claim
  2. The yearly return on the policy
  3. A guaranteed refund of premiums
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: An amount the policyholder pays toward a claim. The excess is the policyholder’s contribution to a covered claim under the policy.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why a person might willingly pay a higher insurance premium for a lower excess.

See a model response

A lower excess reduces the amount paid out of pocket when making a covered claim. Someone with limited emergency savings may prefer that certainty even if the annual premium is higher.

09Personal financeMortgages explainedA loan to buy property, secured against it.+
Learning goal

Understand mortgages explained, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

A mortgage is a property loan secured against the property being purchased. The borrower owes the principal and usually pays interest on the outstanding balance. If repayments cannot be met, the lender may ultimately enforce the security under applicable law.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Principal-and-interest repayments gradually reduce debt. Interest-only arrangements may lower payments initially while leaving principal unchanged. Fixed rates lock in an agreed rate for a period; variable rates can change. Loan term, deposit size, fees and comparison rates all affect total cost.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Test whether the loan remains manageable if rates or expenses rise. A larger deposit can reduce borrowing and sometimes avoid lenders mortgage insurance, but buying also brings stamp duty, maintenance, insurance and other costs.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Borrow $400,000 at a hypothetical 6% per year. The first month’s interest is roughly $400,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = $2,000. A $2,400 principal-and-interest payment would reduce the principal by about $400 in that first month, ignoring fees and exact daily-interest calculations.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A mortgage payment is not all interest, and a lower monthly payment can mean a much higher lifetime interest cost if the loan runs longer.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which loan feature usually lowers the initial monthly repayment but slows principal reduction?

  1. Principal-and-interest repayments
  2. Interest-only repayments
  3. A larger deposit with the same rate
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Interest-only repayments. Interest-only payments do not pay down principal during the interest-only period.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why comparing home loans using only the advertised interest rate can be misleading.

See a model response

Fees, rate conditions, loan term and features alter the total cost. Two loans with similar rates can produce different repayments and lifetime interest, so borrowers should compare total repayments and relevant comparison rates.

01Personal financeNet worthWhat you own minus what you owe.+
Learning goal

Understand net worth, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Net worth is a snapshot of financial position: the value of assets minus outstanding liabilities. Assets might include bank balances, shares, superannuation and a home; liabilities include home loans, credit card debt and other loans. It is not the same as income: a high earner can still have negative net worth.

02 / LEARN

How it works

To calculate it, choose one date, estimate the current value of each asset and subtract the amount owed on each debt. Use conservative estimates for items that are difficult to sell, and do not count future wages as an asset. If a home is included at its market value, also include the mortgage still owing.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Tracking net worth over time can reveal whether saving, investing and debt repayment are building financial resilience. A negative balance is not automatically a failure: someone early in their working life may have education debt and few assets. Cash available for bills still matters, even when net worth is high.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A student has $3,000 in savings, $2,000 invested and $1,000 of credit card debt. Assets total $5,000. Liabilities total $1,000. Net worth = $5,000 − $1,000 = $4,000.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Do not treat an illiquid asset, such as a property, as money immediately available to meet next week’s bills.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which change increases net worth, all else equal?

  1. Taking out a $1,000 loan and keeping the cash
  2. Paying $500 of debt with $500 of savings
  3. Receiving a $500 gift in cash
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Receiving a $500 gift in cash. The gift adds an asset without creating a liability. Borrowing cash adds equal assets and debt; paying debt with savings reduces both equally.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why a household can have positive net worth but struggle to pay its electricity bill.

See a model response

Net worth measures assets less liabilities, not how quickly those assets can be turned into cash. A household may own an expensive home but have little cash in its transaction account. It can therefore have positive net worth and still face a short-term cash-flow problem.

51Personal financeRenting and buyingCompare the full costs and risks of housing choices.+
Learning goal

Understand renting and buying, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Renting provides access to housing without a large purchase deposit, but payments generally do not build equity in the property. Buying may build equity and provide greater housing security, but requires substantial upfront funds and brings ownership costs and risks.

02 / LEARN

How it works

A meaningful comparison includes rent, mortgage interest and principal, maintenance, rates, insurance, purchase duties, selling costs, deposit opportunity cost and potential price changes. Principal repayments are not the same as interest: they convert cash into housing equity, although they still affect monthly affordability.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

The right choice depends on financial resilience, location, expected time in the property and personal needs. Renters may value mobility; owners may value stability. Neither outcome is universally superior, and housing markets can move in either direction.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A buyer pays $2,500 per month on a mortgage: $1,800 interest and $700 principal in a particular month. The whole $2,500 affects cash flow, but only $1,800 is interest expense; the $700 reduces the outstanding debt. Compare this with rent only after including ownership costs and risks.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Do not compare monthly rent with mortgage repayments alone while ignoring rates, repairs, transaction costs and the opportunity cost of the deposit.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which mortgage repayment component directly reduces outstanding loan principal?

  1. Interest
  2. Principal repayment
  3. Property insurance
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Principal repayment. Principal repayments pay down the amount borrowed and build equity, assuming property value stays unchanged.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Evaluate one advantage of renting and one advantage of buying for a young worker.

See a model response

Renting may be preferable for flexibility and lower upfront costs, particularly when work location is uncertain. Buying can offer tenure stability and build housing equity over time, but has higher entry costs and exposes the owner to property-price risk.

45Personal financeSaving and emergency fundsBuild a buffer for unexpected costs.+
Learning goal

Understand saving and emergency funds, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Saving means setting aside income for a future purpose rather than spending it immediately. An emergency fund is money kept accessible for unexpected essential costs or income interruptions. It provides a buffer so that a minor setback does not automatically create expensive debt.

02 / LEARN

How it works

A practical starting point is to save a small target, then build toward an amount that reflects your essential bills and income stability. Many guides mention several months of essential expenses, but suitable buffers vary widely. Accessible savings are generally more appropriate for emergencies than volatile assets.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Automating transfers after payday reduces the need for repeated willpower. Keeping a separate account helps distinguish savings for planned purchases from genuinely unexpected events. Refill the buffer after using it.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A person has $1,400 of essential expenses per month. A two-month emergency buffer would be $2,800. If they already have $800 and save $200 a month, they can reach $2,800 in 10 months, ignoring interest.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

An emergency fund is not the same as money reserved for a known bill such as annual registration; plan for both.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which asset is usually most suitable for an emergency fund?

  1. Accessible savings account
  2. A highly speculative token
  3. A collectable that may take months to sell
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Accessible savings account. Emergency savings need to be relatively stable and accessible when unexpected costs arise.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why emergency savings can reduce the long-term cost of a car repair.

See a model response

Cash savings let the household pay an urgent repair without taking high-interest credit. Avoiding interest and late charges prevents the original repair bill from becoming more expensive over time.

53Personal financeScamsSpot warning signs and verify requests safely.+
Learning goal

Understand scams, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Financial scams use deception to persuade people to transfer money, reveal sensitive details or grant account access. Scammers may impersonate a bank, a government agency, a friend or a convincing investment provider. Urgency and secrecy are common tactics.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Pause before acting, verify the sender through a separately sourced official channel and never share one-time passwords or account access codes. Be suspicious of guaranteed profits, unexpected requests to move money and pressure to bypass normal procedures.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

If you suspect a scam, contact your bank using the number in its official app or website, secure affected accounts and report the incident to appropriate Australian authorities such as Scamwatch. Fast action may reduce harm, though recovery is not guaranteed.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A message claims your bank account will close in 10 minutes and asks you to follow a link. Instead of opening it, launch the bank’s official app or call its publicly listed number. The urgency is a reason to verify independently, not a reason to rush.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A message appearing in a real bank thread or displaying a familiar logo is not proof that it is genuine.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which response is safest when an unexpected caller asks for a one-time banking code?

  1. Read the code to the caller
  2. End the call and contact the bank through its official channel
  3. Reply to the caller’s text to check
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: End the call and contact the bank through its official channel. One-time codes are used to authorise access or transactions and should not be shared with unexpected callers.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain how a student could check an unexpected “investment opportunity” without relying on the promoter’s claims.

See a model response

They should independently research the provider and product, verify any claimed licence through official registers, scrutinise promised returns and seek impartial guidance before transferring money or supplying personal details.

03Personal financeSetting financial goalsClear goals make saving easier to stick to.+
Learning goal

Understand setting financial goals, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

A financial goal describes what money is for, how much is needed and when it is needed. “Save more” is vague; “save $1,200 for a laptop by next June” gives a target and a deadline. Goals are easier to act on when they reflect something personally important.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Work backwards from the deadline. Divide the amount still needed by the number of pay periods available, then automate a transfer if possible. Separate a short-term goal from longer-term goals such as investing or retirement. Account for price changes and leave some room for unexpected expenses.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Revisit goals rather than treating them as promises that can never change. A shift in income, housing or study needs may make a plan unrealistic. A useful plan can be adjusted without losing sight of the objective.

WORKED EXAMPLE

You want $1,500 in 10 months and already have $300. The gap is $1,200. Saving $120 per month reaches the target before interest. If you can save only $80, either allow 15 months, lower the cost or find extra income.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

An ambitious goal is not useful if it requires skipping essential expenses or using expensive debt.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which is the most actionable savings goal?

  1. Save more one day
  2. Save $900 for a course in nine months
  3. Be good with money
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Save $900 for a course in nine months. The target gives a specific amount, purpose and deadline, so monthly savings can be calculated.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Write a plan for a $600 goal over six months when you already have $120.

See a model response

The remaining amount is $480. Saving $80 each month for six months reaches $600, assuming no interest or withdrawals. I would schedule an $80 transfer after payday and review the balance monthly.

06Personal financeSimple vs compound interestCompound interest earns interest on interest.+
Learning goal

Understand simple vs compound interest, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Simple interest is calculated on the original principal each period. Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus previously credited interest. Both are usually described with an annual rate, but the compounding schedule determines how often interest is added.

02 / LEARN

How it works

With simple annual interest, amount = P(1 + rn). With annual compounding, amount = P(1 + r)^n, where P is the starting principal, r is the annual rate and n is the number of years. Compounding can grow savings faster, but it also makes unpaid debt more expensive.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

The difference is small at first and grows over longer periods. Compare accounts using effective rates, fees, restrictions and whether interest is credited monthly or daily. For debts, focus on the total cost and repayment schedule, not just the advertised rate.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Invest $1,000 at 5% for three years. Simple interest produces $1,000 × (1 + 0.05 × 3) = $1,150. Annual compounding produces $1,000 × 1.05³ ≈ $1,157.63, or $7.63 more.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Compounding is not a guaranteed benefit: interest rates can change, and fees or inflation reduce real gains.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Under compound interest, what happens to interest earned in earlier periods?

  1. It is discarded
  2. It becomes part of the balance that can earn more interest
  3. It is always paid out in cash
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: It becomes part of the balance that can earn more interest. Credited interest becomes part of the balance, so later interest may be earned on it.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why two accounts offering the same advertised annual rate might produce different balances.

See a model response

If one account compounds more frequently, credited interest begins earning further interest sooner. Differences in fees and rate conditions can also alter the final balance, so the effective return matters more than the headline rate.

46Personal financeSuperannuationUnderstand how Australian retirement savings work.+
Learning goal

Understand superannuation, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Superannuation is Australia’s long-term retirement saving system. Eligible employers generally make contributions into a nominated super fund, where money is invested. Contributions, investment returns, fees, insurance and tax all affect the final balance.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Super funds commonly offer different investment options, ranging from defensive to growth-focused. Over a long horizon, compounding can be powerful, but investment values can fall. Most super money cannot be freely withdrawn before legal conditions of release are satisfied.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Young workers should check that employers are paying correctly and ensure contact details and beneficiaries are current. Compare fees and insurance before combining accounts, because consolidation may remove cover. Use current ATO guidance for contribution and withdrawal rules.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A hypothetical $5,000 in super earns an average net return of 5% a year for 20 years with no further contributions. Its simplified future value is $5,000 × 1.05²⁰ ≈ $13,266. Actual returns, fees and tax will vary.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Superannuation is invested retirement savings, not a bank account with a guaranteed annual return.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

What is the main purpose of superannuation?

  1. Funding everyday purchases next week
  2. Building retirement savings
  3. Avoiding all investment risk
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Building retirement savings. The super system is primarily designed to accumulate savings for retirement, subject to access rules.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain why fund fees matter especially to a young person with decades before retirement.

See a model response

01 / LEARN

The concept

Money received today can be used immediately or invested, so the same nominal amount received in the future is generally worth less today. This is called the time value of money. Inflation is one reason; the opportunity to earn a return and the risk of non-payment also matter.

02 / LEARN

WORKED EXAMPLE

Do not assume a higher future dollar amount is always better; the timing and the discount rate change the comparison.

Learning goal

Understand global central banks, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Central banks influence interest rates and financial conditions in their own economies. Decisions by the US Federal Reserve, European Central Bank and other central banks can affect global borrowing costs, capital flows and Australia’s exchange rate.

02 / LEARN

How it works

If overseas interest rates rise relative to Australian rates, some investors may find foreign-currency assets more attractive, placing downward pressure on the AUD, all else equal. However, exchange rates also reflect expectations, commodity prices and risk, so the outcome is not mechanical.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Global monetary tightening can weaken demand for Australian exports and make international financing more expensive. The RBA assesses these spillovers but sets domestic monetary policy for Australian economic conditions and its statutory objectives.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Suppose Australian short-term rates are unchanged while US rates rise. US assets may offer a higher relative yield, encouraging some capital flows toward the US and potentially weakening the AUD. An unexpected shift in commodity prices could offset this effect.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Overseas rate increases do not force the RBA to match them; domestic inflation, employment and financial conditions also matter.

35GlobalOil and fuel pricesOil is a key input to transport and production.+
Learning goal

Understand oil and fuel prices, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Crude oil is traded globally and used to make transport fuels, chemicals and many industrial products. Changes in world oil prices can affect petrol prices, freight costs and business production costs. Local taxes, exchange rates, refining and retail margins also influence the pump price.

02 / LEARN

How it works

A negative oil supply shock, such as disrupted production, can shift global oil supply left and raise prices. Higher transport and input costs can then shift the short-run aggregate supply curve left in importing economies, pushing prices upward and output downward.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

The effect is uneven: drivers and airlines face higher costs, while some energy producers may earn higher revenue. Central banks face a difficult trade-off when inflation rises even as economic activity weakens.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Suppose a delivery business uses 2,000 litres of fuel each month. If its fuel price rises from $1.80 to $2.10 per litre, the direct monthly fuel bill rises by $600. The firm might absorb the cost, charge customers more or reduce other spending.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A petrol price increase is not necessarily evidence that domestic consumer demand grew; the shock may come from global supply or the exchange rate.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which curve most directly shifts when higher fuel costs make firms’ production more expensive?

  1. Short-run aggregate supply to the left
  2. Short-run aggregate supply to the right
  3. Household demand to the right
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Short-run aggregate supply to the left. Higher economy-wide input costs reduce how much firms can profitably supply at each price level.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Analyse one reason a rise in world oil prices could increase inflation while slowing growth.

See a model response

Higher oil prices raise transport and production costs, which firms may pass on as higher prices. Those costs also squeeze real household income and business margins, reducing consumption or output.

36GlobalSupply chainsThe network that moves goods from maker to buyer.+
Learning goal

Understand supply chains, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

A supply chain links the stages needed to produce and deliver a good, from raw materials and components to factories, warehouses, shops and customers. Many products depend on inputs from several countries, so a disruption far away can affect local prices and availability.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Delays or shortages of one critical input can limit output even when other inputs are plentiful. Firms may respond by holding additional inventory, finding alternative suppliers, redesigning products or moving production. These responses improve resilience but can raise costs.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

“Just in time” inventory can reduce storage expenses, yet provides less buffer when deliveries stop. Supply-chain choices involve a trade-off between efficiency under normal conditions and resilience during disruptions.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A bicycle maker can assemble 100 bicycles a week but receives only 60 gear sets after a shipping disruption. Even with enough frames and wheels, maximum production falls to 60 bicycles unless substitute gear sets can be sourced.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A business using more suppliers is not automatically risk-free if those suppliers depend on the same port, region or raw material.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

  1. Output may be constrained despite other inputs being available
  2. All other inputs automatically become cheaper
  3. Its labour productivity must rise
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Write it like an economist

Explain the trade-off between holding more inventory and using just-in-time delivery.

See a model response

Extra inventory increases storage and financing costs but offers protection against delayed shipments. Just-in-time systems reduce normal operating costs but can leave production vulnerable when supply chains are disrupted.

Learning goal

Understand command words, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

A command word indicates the cognitive work an assessment expects. Define requires an accurate meaning; explain requires a causal account; analyse examines relationships and patterns; evaluate makes a justified judgement using criteria. The exact wording and marking standards depend on the subject and assessment.

02 / LEARN

How it works

For explanation questions, state the mechanism explicitly rather than merely describing events. For analysis, use data to establish what changed, how much and why that matters. For evaluation, choose relevant criteria—such as efficiency, equity or sustainability—and compare outcomes before reaching a supported judgement.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Marks are not allocated by counting how many times you use a key verb. Read any supplied stimulus, scope and criterion carefully; then plan the depth and structure of evidence needed. The current QCAA Economics syllabus and task-specific instrument should take priority over generic advice.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Compare: “The tariff was 25%” merely describes a fact. “The tariff increased the landed cost, reducing competitiveness” explains a mechanism. “Imports fell 30% after the change, although exchange rates also moved” analyses an observed result with a causal qualification.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

An evaluation does not require equal praise and criticism; it requires a decision defended against suitable criteria.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which response best fits the command word “evaluate”?

  1. List three economic policies
  2. Define the term tariff
  3. Judge the policy against equity and efficiency criteria using evidence
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Judge the policy against equity and efficiency criteria using evidence. Evaluation calls for a supported judgement using relevant criteria, not simply a list or definition.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Explain the difference between analysing and evaluating a rise in rental prices.

See a model response

Analysis identifies the trend and examines causes and consequences—for example, how inelastic housing supply and population growth affect rents. Evaluation then judges a policy response using criteria such as affordability, efficiency, equity and implementation time.

41Study skillsDrawing diagramsLabelled diagrams earn marks.+
Learning goal

Understand drawing diagrams, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

An economic diagram is a compact explanation of a relationship. For a standard supply-and-demand graph, put price (P) on the vertical axis and quantity (Q) on the horizontal axis. Label the original curves, intersection and equilibrium price and quantity clearly.

02 / LEARN

How it works

When a non-price determinant changes, draw a new curve shifted in the correct direction, such as D₀ to D₁. Mark the new intersection and use guide lines to compare P₀ with P₁ and Q₀ with Q₁. If a price changes without any shift, show a movement along the original curve instead.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Diagrams become persuasive when they match the written mechanism and its assumptions. Show which curve shifts first, explain why, then state who gains or loses. In tariff models, distinguish world price, tariff-inclusive price, imports and welfare effects.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A rise in consumer tastes for a normal good shifts demand right from D₀ to D₁. With an unchanged upward-sloping S, the new intersection lies higher and further right. Therefore P₁ > P₀ and Q₁ > Q₀. The diagram should show both changes.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A beautifully drawn diagram can still be economically wrong if the curve moves for the wrong reason or labels contradict the explanation.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

A higher input cost affects which curve in the standard product market?

  1. Demand shifts right
  2. Supply shifts left
  3. Demand moves down along itself
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Supply shifts left. Higher input costs make production less profitable at each price, so market supply shifts left.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Describe how to diagram a fall in the price of a product when supply increases.

See a model response

Draw initial downward-sloping demand D₀ and upward-sloping supply S₀. Shift supply right to S₁, identify the new intersection E₁, and label a lower equilibrium price P₁ and higher quantity Q₁ relative to P₀ and Q₀.

38Study skillsPEEL paragraphsPoint, Evidence, Explain, Link.+
Learning goal

Understand peel paragraphs, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

PEEL is one useful way to organise an explanatory paragraph: Point, Evidence, Explain and Link. It is a planning tool, not an official marking scheme or a requirement to use four sentences. Strong economic writing prioritises accurate causal reasoning and relevant evidence over a rigid formula.

02 / LEARN

How it works

A point answers the exact question. Evidence supplies a figure, a relevant event or an economic model. Explanation connects the evidence to the claim using a clear mechanism; the link shows why the argument matters to the question. Some tasks require more than one piece of evidence or a qualified judgement.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

When editing, remove sentences that only repeat the question. Ask whether every statistic has been interpreted and whether the paragraph names the direction of change and affected stakeholders. A concise paragraph can contain several layers of reasoning without sounding formulaic.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Question: Explain why a tariff can reduce imports. Point: A tariff raises the domestic price of imported goods. Evidence: In a small-country model, price rises from Pw to Pw + t. Explain: Domestic consumers buy less and local firms supply more at the higher price. Link: The gap between demand and supply narrows, so import quantity contracts.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Do not add “this shows” after every sentence. Use that space to explain the causal connection the evidence supports.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which part of PEEL most directly establishes why the evidence supports your point?

  1. Evidence
  2. Explanation
  3. Heading
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: Explanation. Explanation connects the example or data to the economic mechanism and the claim.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Improve this weak sentence: “Tariffs are bad because they affect trade.”

See a model response

A tariff can reduce import volumes by raising domestic prices: consumers purchase less while local producers increase supply, narrowing the import gap. The magnitude depends on how much of the duty is passed through and on demand and supply elasticities.

40Study skillsReading data questionsUse the data in your answer.+
Learning goal

Understand reading data questions, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

Data questions test whether you can extract relevant evidence and reason from it. Start by reading the title, units, period, geographic coverage and source. Check whether values are nominal or real, totals or percentages, and whether the graph uses a truncated axis.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Describe patterns with start and end values, changes, comparisons and anomalies. Then explain mechanisms without claiming that correlation proves causation. Use “percentage points” for the difference between two percentages, and “per cent” for a proportional change relative to the starting value.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

If the question asks for a judgement, consider limitations: missing years, an unclear definition, seasonal effects and other factors that may explain the trend. A source’s usefulness depends on the question it is used to answer.

WORKED EXAMPLE

The unemployment rate rises from 4% to 5%. That is an increase of 1 percentage point, or a 25% proportional rise in the unemployment rate ((5 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100). Those statements are both accurate but describe different changes.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

Do not infer the reason a series changed simply because a policy occurred around the same date. Identify other plausible influences.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

A rate rises from 6% to 9%. The change in percentage points is…

  1. 3 percentage points
  2. 50 percentage points
  3. 3% of its starting level
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: 3 percentage points. 9 minus 6 is a 3-percentage-point increase; the proportional rise is 50%.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Analyse the statement: “Exports rose from $200 million to $240 million, proving a tariff helped exporters.”

See a model response

Export value rose $40 million, or 20%, but this alone cannot establish the tariff’s effect. Higher prices, exchange rates, diversion to other markets or other demand changes could also explain the increase. Export quantities and a comparison period would help test the claim.

42Study skillsWriting evaluationsWeigh both sides and decide.+
Learning goal

Understand writing evaluations, explain how it works and apply it to an economic or financial decision.

01 / LEARN

The concept

An evaluation answers “How good is this outcome or policy, and for whom?” Rather than listing pros and cons, choose criteria that fit the decision. In economics, criteria often include efficiency, equity, effectiveness, sustainability and feasibility.

02 / LEARN

How it works

Compare the magnitude, timing and distribution of likely effects. Use evidence to support competing claims and recognise uncertainty. A strong judgement is conditional where necessary: a policy may be useful in the short run but fail to address the underlying supply problem.

03 / LEARN

Why it matters

Organise the response around a defensible argument. State a criterion, present the most relevant evidence, explain trade-offs and decide which consideration carries more weight. Avoid generic conclusions such as “there are advantages and disadvantages”.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Question: Evaluate rent assistance during a housing shortage. Against equity, assistance can support lower-income tenants immediately. Against long-run efficiency, its impact may be limited if the supply of affordable homes cannot expand. Overall, it can relieve present stress but works best alongside measures that improve supply.

COMMON MISCONCEPTION

A balanced evaluation does not mean both sides must receive equal weight. State clearly which option you support and on what evidence.

04 / CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Which conclusion demonstrates an evaluation?

  1. “There are positive and negative impacts.”
  2. “Assistance is preferable for immediate relief, but added supply is needed for lasting affordability.”
  3. “Rent assistance is a payment.”
Reveal the answer and reasoning

Answer: “Assistance is preferable for immediate relief, but added supply is needed for lasting affordability.”. The second conclusion weighs outcomes by timing and explains a reasoned preference.

05 / APPLY YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Write it like an economist

Evaluate a subsidy for electric buses using two economic criteria.

See a model response

A subsidy may improve environmental sustainability by reducing local emissions and can improve equity if it expands affordable public transport. However, the cost to taxpayers and opportunity cost of other spending reduce its efficiency unless benefits exceed costs. I would support a targeted subsidy where expected public benefits are demonstrably large.

Why financial literacy matters

Financial literacy is not a hobby for people who enjoy money. It is the working knowledge that lets you read a loan contract, compare two offers and tell a sound plan from a sales pitch. In a year when the cash rate has moved 125 basis points, understanding the difference between a variable and a fixed rate can be worth hundreds of dollars a month.

It matters for the economy too. Monetary policy works through households: a rate decision changes spending only if people notice and respond to borrowing costs, saving returns and inflation. Citizens who can reason about money are better placed to separate signal from noise in booms and busts.

And it is a civic skill. Budgets, tax settings, superannuation, housing and energy policy all rest on numbers. Literacy lets you read a claim, ask what it is measured against, and form your own view, whichever side you land on.
